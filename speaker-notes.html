Where do we draw the line?

On legitimate uses of AI and open information commons, and commitment to community values

Author
Affiliation

Zachary Batist

McGill University

Researchers share their work openly.
AI consumes it at scale.
Is this a new problem, or an old one made visible?

I want to start with a provocation rather than a thesis.

The tension between open scholarship and AI is real — but I don’t think it’s new.

☞ I think AI has made visible a set of tensions that were always present in how we think about open information commons.

This talk is about those tensions, and what a social rather than technical lens reveals about them.

In other words, there is a lot of data in the commons, which can be used in many, many ways — some more helpful than others.

So this talk is about AI only inasmuch as it is about access to the commons.

I’d also like to point out that I’m addressing these concerns as a researcher, not as a maintainer of research infrastructure. Others, especially during the first session, addressed how challenges were experienced on that end, i.e. how to deal with bot swarms, how to think about sensitive datasets being shared openly, etc.

☞

Commons are social systems

Researchers have always participated in information commons:

  • Citing prior work, sharing data, building on others’ findings
  • These commons are scaffolded by norms and commitments — not just platforms and mandates
  • Instilled through participation in communities of practice

Science reform operates by implementing new rules, new laws, new technical processes.

So what makes that kind of engagement possible? And what threatens it?

☞ Researchers have always participated in information commons — citing, sharing, building on prior work.

☞ But these commons are scaffolded by norms and commitments instilled through community membership, not just platforms or mandates.

☞ However, we are increasingly being told that opening our research requires new rules, new laws, and new technical processes - which may contradict or disrupt established norms and traditions.

While some significant changes may be warranted, they must be implemented with care.

☞

Commons are social systems

Technical camp

Infrastructures & mandates
Information as disembodied
Problems are legalistic in nature

Humanistic camp

Reforming science as inclusive
Knowledge as socially situated
Problems are rooted in culture

The technical camp dominates policy.
The humanistic camp is better equipped to understand the actual problem.

☞ The open science movement has two orientations that rarely talk to each other. A technical camp designs infrastructure and mandates — FAIR data, open access requirements, licensing frameworks, whereas a humanistic camp is concerned with reforming science as a more inclusive, culturally aware enterprise.

The technical camp sees problems as legalistic: access, interoperability, reproducibility, and the humanistic camp sees problems as rooted in culture: who gets to participate, whose knowledge counts, how norms are negotiated.

☞ The technical camp dominates policy.

☞ But the humanistic camp is better equipped to understand the actual problems — which is why we’re here today talking about AI and commons rather than just filing a complaint with a licensing body.

But before I continue, I want to be upfront about where I’m coming from.

I do use AI in my work. I use commercial tools to develop data processing pipelines that I would not otherwise be able to make, I use local models as part of extended text analysis toolchains, and I generally find it handy as a reflexive tool to enter in dialog with myself.

But I also think it’s important to do these things with care and with critical thought.

I use these tools as methods, not as sources of truth, and as I’ll argue here today, this makes a world of a difference.

☞

The “no boundaries” claim is a happy lie

“Universal access” sounds egalitarian.

But even the most committed open advocates expect citation, attribution, appropriate reuse.

Commons have never been, and don’t need to be, egalitarian.
They are governed — who contributes, who accesses, in what ways.

The boundary is not a wall. It’s a sniff test —
judged by behavior, care, and commitment, not credentials alone.

That distinction between using tools as methods, with care, in compliance with community norms and values, is exactly what open science policy tends to flatten.

It declares “universal access” as if no conditions exist, but they always have — they’re just implicit.

☞ Even open science’s most committed advocates claim ownership of their creative outputs, and expect their work to be cited, engaged with charitably, and used in ways consistent with its intent.

☞ In this sense, open commons have always been ☞ governed.

We actually impose strict controls over who may contribute and extract from the commons, and in what ways.

☞ The mechanism for enforcing these rules are rarely formal. It’s more of a sensibility —

☞ a judgment about whether someone is engaging in good faith, with care, with some understanding of what they’re working with. Credentials don’t confer that. Community membership does.

☞

Case 1: PDF piracy

Aaron Swartz
Shared academic papers widely
→ Celebrated

Meta
Scraped LibGen at scale
→ Condemned

Same act. Radically different valence.

One serves the community — makes research available to people who can’t get it any other way, and adds to the commons in doing so.

The other extracts from it — a predatory actor in it for themselves alone.

The difference is relational, not technical or legal.

One example is the different ways we address PDF piracy.

☞ Aaron Swartz famously downloaded millions of academic papers and planned to release them publicly. The research community celebrated him for it.

☞ Meta scraped LibGen to train its models — an outside actor with nothing invested in the commons, and the community condemned them for it.

☞ They engaged in the same act, but with radically different valence.

☞ ☞ Swartz served the community — made research available to people who couldn’t get it any other way, and in this way, he added to the commons.

☞ ☞ Meta extracts from it — a predatory actor with no stake in what it takes.

☞ ☞ The difference isn’t technical, or even legal. It’s relational.

☞

Case 2: The Internet Archive

The National Emergency Library (2020) — granting access to books during Covid-19.

  • Legitimate, community-serving initiative ✓
  • Driven by confidence in technical solutions to social problems ✗

The manner of the intervention mattered as much as the outcome.

The Internet Archive’s National Emergency Library is a more complicated case.

During the Covid-19 pandemic, it set out to make books more readily available as a public service.

☞ ☞ Their mission was legitimate and community-serving, even if it did not entirely adhere with professional archival and legal principles.

☞ ☞ But it was also an example of a certain tech-driven confidence: the idea that you can resolve questions of access, copyright, and distribution through unilateral technical action.

It ended in lawsuits that set legal precedent for how similar actions may proceed in the future, which complicates the IA’s identity as a leading actor in the archival community.

☞ ☞ The point isn’t that they were wrong to try. It’s that the manner of the intervention mattered as much as the outcome.

☞

Case 3: Big data archaeology

Archaeology produces a lot of data.
Much of it is now publicly available.

What happens when outsiders use it?

One final example is the reuse of archaeological research data.

Archaeology produces a lot of data, and much of it is now publicly available.

☞ Does the manner of its reuse matter? What happens when non-archaeologists try to use it?

☞

Case 3: Big data archaeology

Computer scientists / data scientists / physicists publish high-profile papers “solving” archaeological problems with large integrated datasets.

Outside the community Inside the community
Treatment of data Neutral, abstract, recombinant Storied, situated, context-laden
Epistemic stance Data taken as source of universal truth Contributing one point of view
Community response Intuitively rejected Treated with consideration

Understanding a dataset’s circumstances of creation comes from community membership.

The difference is not credentials, but care.

I think we have all been frustrated when a computer scientists, data scientist of physicist has tried to apply a nonsensical approach using open humanities data.

☞ I don’t know about you, but most archaeologists I know who read these outsider papers will feel something is wrong before they can fully articulate why.

This is because the data is being treated as neutral and abstract — as if knowing the variable names is the same as knowing what they mean.

☞ Archaeologists know that datasets carry histories. They know that a field record reflects the decisions of a particular excavator on a particular day, under particular conditions. That knowledge comes from having been trained as an archaeologist.

And archaeologists are very keen to welcome cross-disciplinary collaborations, which — when done right, essentially entails communicating these values across the disciplinary boundary.

☞ In other words, it’s not really about credentials, but care.

☞

AI as amplifier, not origin

We put our data out there. We made it FAIR.
We imagined collegial use-cases, and engaging with others who care.

Putting data on the open web has always involved relinquishing control.

The problem isn’t AI per se, but how data is deployed beyond community membership — without the gradual participation that builds the tacit knowledge to use data responsibly.

Overall, I don’t think we can really blame others for these problems.

We did this. We built the infrastructure, we put our data out there and declared it open.

The FAIR principles — Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable — were designed with certain kinds of users in mind.

Maybe language model training pipelines, built by those who actually care.

☞ But putting machine-readable data on the open web without restriction has always involved relinquishing control.

☞ ☞ AI didn’t break the commons, but it did expose the gap between our expectations of careful use and how others will actually use the data we make available.

☞

Is AI being done by humanists,
or by engineers playing humanist
for an afternoon?

A blanket boycott treats all AI use as equivalent — which is exactly the kind of category error I’ve been arguing against throughout this talk.

The question isn’t whether AI is involved, but who is doing the work, how it’s being done, whether it’s done with respect and care for the data and the community, and how truth claims are expressed.

For example, using local models targeting a specific research question, with explicit attention to its limitations looks very different from training a foundation model on scraped scholarship and selling access to it.

☞

Where do we draw the line?

Re-defining open social scholarship is not about redrawing the line from scratch.

It’s about making the existing lines more legible —
more porous where appropriate,
defended where they matter.

These boundaries do and should continue to exist, and it is our responsibly to tend them.

The conference theme asks us to re-define open social scholarship.

I want to resist the word “re-define” slightly — not because definitions don’t matter, but because the framing implies we’re starting from scratch.

We’re not. We have communities of practice. We have norms. We have commitments. AI has strained them, but it hasn’t erased them.

☞ What’s needed is not new definitions but greater legibility —

☞ making explicit the implicit agreements that have always governed how we share and use scholarly work.

This also involves softening the boundaries in some places, and defending them in others.

☞ In other words, these boundaries do and should continue to exist, and it is our responsibly to tend to them.

Someone asked about how we know when we’re doing AI right, and inasmuch as this is a talk about commons which has implications for AI, I’ll take the liberty of extending this to be about data applications.

None of us has to, or should, make such determinations on our own. We are in constant dialog. Values are not held individually but are internalized by involvement in a community of practice. Being able to talk openly and freely, and casually and candidly, matters a lot. Being able to talk outside the boundaries of institutional channels, being able to explore subtext and context as gatherings such as this, through informal, casual and kind discussion, is important.

Not gotchas, but learning experiences.

So to address my initial question, the line is not a strict boundary, it’s a path we straddle as we move along it and weave across it. It’s not fixed in place, but defined in part by our movements and our changing political, social and material environments. I have faith that we can not only figure it out, but take a leading role in guiding how others navigate this complex space.
